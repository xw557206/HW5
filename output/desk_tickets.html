Reflection · what actually happened

Where things stand as I write this. All three tickets ran against the live model and the real database. None of them is resolved: 101 is blocked, 102 is waiting_approval, and 103 is still open in the database even though the run concluded it was blocked. One approval ($2,400 rent) is pending my decision, one customer draft exists, no payment has been made, and the balance is unchanged at $3,400. Everything below is based on those records, not on how I hoped it would go.

Question 1

How well did the agents handle each ticket?

I would say the agents did a nice job following the rules of this shop. That part I am genuinely happy with. Where they struggled was everything after the rules — actually finishing the job.

Ticket 102 — the best example

Ticket 102 was the best example of how I wanted it to run. Facilities checked the rent payment, confirmed there was cash on the books, and then put in the request for approval. No transaction or payment was made in the process.

What the record shows. Five tool calls, in order: get_ticket, check_rent_due, get_cash_position, request_approval, update_ticket_status. Approval #1 was created with requires_human_approval: true and the agent stopped there. The balance is still $3,400.00 and the payments table is empty.

Ticket 101 — did not do as well

Ticket 101 was not doing great compared to ticket 102. The service correctly identified that the Bulldog tee was not in stock, and a response was drafted. But the agents failed to take the next step and address the unpaid vendor invoice, which caused the restocking to fail.

What the record shows. Customer Service confirmed size S at qty 0 and wrote draft #1. No agent called request_approval on this ticket, so the $840 invoice #501 — the only thing standing between the shop and a restock — was never put in front of a human. The shop could have afforded it: $3,400 on hand against $840.

I could also see that the leadership used the incorrect SKU when quality-controlling inventory, but still accepted the wrong result. That made me question the accuracy and reliability of the review process.

What the record shows. The Boss called check_order_fulfillment({"sku": "bulldog_tee_white"}). The real SKU is CC-TEE-WHITE. The tool returned product_name: null and qty_on_hand: 0 — which reads exactly like a genuine out-of-stock — and the Boss treated it as confirmation of Customer Service’s finding. The conclusion happened to be right, but the check that was supposed to verify it did not actually verify anything.

Ticket 103 — the worst performer

Ticket 103 was the worst performer. Even though the accounting department communicated the inventory problem, inventory got the shortage calculation wrong, and the ticket was not updated.

What the record shows. Accounting called ask_inventory about restocking 12 units. Inventory replied “the shelf has 8, leaving a shortfall of 4” — it subtracted the 8 already on the shelf from the 12 still needed. The real shortfall is 12 of 20. The Boss reviewed the run and did not catch it. No agent called update_ticket_status, so the ticket is still open in the database even though the run concluded it was blocked.

Did the right agents get involved?

Broadly yes — each ticket reached an agent whose job it was. The gap was who didn’t get involved. Invoice #501 blocks both 101 and 103, and Accounting is the agent who could have raised a payment request to clear it. On 101 Accounting was never brought in at all. On 103 Accounting was actually leading the ticket and still did not raise it — it reported the blocked vendor as a fact rather than treating it as the thing to fix.

Question 2

Actual workflows against what I expected

I understand how each ticket should be handled, and I expect the leader to delegate based on each agent’s specific capacity — inventory handling to Inventory, the lease and rent to Facilities, quality reviews to whoever is actually positioned to check the work, pricing and cash to Accounting. That is the whole reason for having five agents instead of one.

What I got was closer to a filing system than a manager. The ticket went to whoever owns that ticket type, and then that agent mostly worked alone. The Boss did not look at a ticket and decide who was best placed to handle each part of it; it reviewed afterwards. On 101 that meant the stock question went to Customer Service rather than Inventory, and the invoice question went to nobody at all.

Unexpected delegations

Only one delegation happened in three runs: Accounting calling ask_inventory on 103. I expected delegation to be the normal way these agents worked. In practice they overwhelmingly preferred their own tools, which I now think is the right instinct — a tool call is cheap and a delegation costs a whole model run.

One record is misleading and I want to flag it rather than quietly fix it: the Boss’s audit entry for ticket 102 lists delegated_to: ["facilities"], but the Boss never called an ask_* tool. The orchestrator routed to Facilities; the model reported that routing as its own delegation. The step list in audit_trail.json is accurate — the self-reported field is not.

Unnecessary steps

The Boss reviewing every ticket is the most expensive habit in the system.

Evidence. Across the three tickets, specialists made 12 tool calls doing the work and the Boss made 15 reviewing it. On 102 the Boss re-read the rent, the cash and the approval that Facilities had just created — and reported that everything matched. On 101 its review introduced the only factual error in the run.

So the review stage cost more tool calls than the work itself, caught nothing, and added a mistake. On this evidence I would make the Boss review conditional — on money moving, on a rule being refused, on a specialist reporting low confidence — rather than running it every time.

What matched

The business rules behaved exactly as planned, which is the part I was most worried about. No agent paid anything. No agent tried to approve its own request — they could not, because decide_approval is filtered out of their toolset. Both communications stayed drafts. Every date was judged against 2026-08-31 rather than the real date. And the cash position correctly reported $2,400 committed against a $3,400 balance the moment the approval existed, so the money was treated as spoken for before it moved.

Question 3

Could one agent plus MCP tools have done this better?

Looking back, I don’t think every task necessarily benefited from having multiple agents. While dividing responsibilities makes sense for more complicated problems, it can also create unnecessary communication and additional AI calls.

For example, in Ticket 102, Facilities handled the entire rent review on its own — including checking the lease, confirming the available cash, and submitting the payment for approval. The Boss then went back and checked the same rent, cash balance, and approval request, which felt somewhat repetitive.

What the record shows. Facilities made five tool calls and finished the ticket. The Boss then made four more: get_ticket, check_rent_due, get_cash_position, get_approval — the same three facts Facilities had just established, plus the approval it had just created. Its conclusion was that everything matched.

I think a single Accounting agent with access to the right MCP tools could have handled this ticket just as effectively, without involving Facilities and without requiring another round of review. Of course, having the Boss double-check financial decisions adds some protection, but in this case I’m not sure the additional steps provided enough value to justify the extra complexity.

What the record shows. Across all three tickets the specialists made 12 tool calls doing the work, and the Boss made 15 reviewing it. On 102 the review confirmed what was already correct. On 101 the review introduced the only factual error in the entire run — the invented SKU. So the checking stage cost more than the work, caught nothing, and was itself the least reliable part.

This made me realise that having more agents doesn’t necessarily mean the system will work better, especially for relatively straightforward tasks.

Where the extra agents did earn their place

Ticket 103 is the one case that cuts the other way, at least partly. Accounting genuinely needed vendor information it did not have, and asking Inventory was a reasonable move — that was the only real agent-to-agent call in the whole exercise. But even there, Accounting already had check_vendor_status in its own toolset. One tool call would have done it, and it would have avoided importing Inventory’s arithmetic error.

What I take from this is that the value sits in the tools more than the org chart. get_ticket resolves a ticket and everything it links to in one call; check_order_fulfillment bundles stock, alternatives and vendor standing; quote_bulk_discount returns the whole margin picture. Once a tool answers the real question, the agent calling it barely has to reason. The agents were only as good as the tool they happened to reach for.

If I rebuilt this, I would keep the five agents for the genuinely messy tickets but make the Boss a router and an exception handler rather than a reviewer of everything — triggered by money moving or a rule being refused, not by every ticket that happens to finish.

Question 4

Three problems the team could already handle

The agent team could handle several problems the shop runs into, and the three runs we did already show how each one would be tested.

1. Out-of-stock orders

Inventory could use the check to confirm the stock level and any restock options, and Customer Service can handle the rest of the communication with the customer.

A concrete case: someone asks for the Crest Mug. CC-MUG-CREST sits at qty 0 in Aisle D, but its supplier is Elm City Gifts, who have no unpaid invoices and a 3-day lead.

Why this is a better test than ticket 101. Here the vendor is actually orderable, so the run would reach request_approval for a purchase order and then create_purchase_order — two write tools that no run has exercised yet, because invoice #501 blocked the only apparel vendor every time.

2. Overdue vendor invoices

Accounting should check the invoice and the cash before sending the request for approval for payment.

A concrete case: invoice #501 itself — $840 to Bulldog Print Co, due 2026-08-28, three days overdue. Every tool needed already exists: list_outstanding_invoices and get_cash_position to establish it, request_approval to put it in front of a person.

The awkward part. This is exactly the problem the team already had in front of it on two tickets and never raised. The capability is there; the follow-through was not. At $3,400 the shop could have afforded both this and the rent ($3,240 together, leaving $160).

3. Discount requests

Accounting should review the pricing and margins, and make sure of the inventory level.

A concrete case: the lacrosse team wants 15 caps at a discount. CC-HAT-BLUE has 40 in stock at cost $6, list $22, so quote_bulk_discount returns a $6 floor and a 72.7% maximum discount.

Why it is cleaner than ticket 103. There is no shortfall and no blocked vendor, so the discount is the only question — which is what that tool was built to answer. Ticket 103 stalled because supply and price were tangled together.

But I would want additional checks

These are demonstrated in the existing runs, so I am fairly confident the team could take them on. I would still want additional checks first, given the inventory error that showed up.

Why that matters for all three. Every one of these categories depends on a quantity being correct. Out-of-stock orders need the shortfall right before anything is ordered; discount requests need the available quantity right before a price is agreed; and a purchase order built on a wrong number spends real money on the wrong amount of stock. On ticket 103 Inventory reported a shortfall of 4 when the true figure was 12, and the Boss review did not catch it. Nothing went wrong that time because the vendor was blocked anyway — the error never reached a decision. In the Crest Mug case the vendor is not blocked, so the same mistake would turn straight into a wrong order.

So before trusting these, I would want the shortfall arithmetic computed by the tool and returned as a field rather than worked out by the agent in prose, and I would want any quantity that drives a purchase order checked against the catalogue a second time at the point the order is placed.

Question 5

Three problems it could not handle, and what is missing

1. A customer requests a refund

If a customer requested a refund, Accounting would need a new MCP tool to process refunds and update the payment records. Customer Service can handle the communication.

What is missing There is no refund tool among the 19, and record_payment only ever debits the account — it subtracts from the balance and marks an invoice paid. The payments table has no refund kind and no link to a customer. There is also nothing to refund against: no table records what a named customer bought or what they paid. So Accounting would need process_refund, a customer_orders table to tie it to, and an approval kind of its own — money leaving the shop in the other direction still needs a human.

2. Forecasting future demand

If the shop wanted to forecast future demand, Inventory should have access to historical sales data and a forecasting tool, to better estimate how much stock to order. This would help reduce inventory shortages significantly and avoid running out of stock.

What is missing The shop has no memory. None of the 12 tables holds a sale, a date of sale, or any history at all — inventory is a single snapshot of what is on the shelf right now, and desk holds one date. Inventory would need a sales table with dated line items, then get_sales_history(sku, weeks) and forecast_demand(sku, size, weeks). This is the largest gap of the three: it is not one missing tool, it is a missing dimension — time.

3. A vendor shipment is delayed

If a vendor shipment was delayed, Inventory would need a tracking tool to check the status of the delivery. Customer Service should be notified as well, so that the customer can be told. It helps manage the customer experience and reduces uncertainty.

What is missing purchase_orders records expected_days at the moment an order is placed and then never changes — there is no received date, no carrier, no actual delivery status, and no tool that updates any of it. Inventory would need check_shipment_status(purchase_order_id) and record_delivery(purchase_order_id, received_qty), which would also finally let stock increase when goods arrive. The notification side is the harder half: nothing today pushes anything to another agent. Customer Service only ever acts because the orchestrator called it, so the shop would need an event or a queue — something that says a delivery slipped and a customer is waiting on it.

The pattern behind all three

They are the same shape. The agents are good at reading the present state of the shop and poor at anything else, because almost every tool is a reader. Of the 19 MCP tools only six write, and four of those sit behind human approval. That was a deliberate safety choice and I stand by it — but it means the system can describe a problem far better than it can fix one, which is roughly what tickets 101 and 103 demonstrated.

The other thing all three need is agents actually talking to each other: refunds need Accounting and Customer Service, delays need Inventory and Customer Service. Right now that only happens when one agent explicitly calls another, and across three tickets that happened exactly once.

In summary

My overall verdict

Across all three tickets, the agents seemed to understand their roles and responsibilities, but they weren’t always good at connecting the pieces or following through. They could each describe their part of the problem accurately. What they could not do reliably was carry a finding to the action it implied — spotting the blocking invoice but never asking anyone to pay it, reaching a conclusion but never writing it back to the ticket.

The system is overall a good starting point, but I wouldn’t feel comfortable letting it run the website on its own, without quality checks.

The last thing I took away is that adding more agents does not necessarily solve a problem if they do not communicate with each other. Five agents with clear job descriptions still left the invoice unaddressed on two tickets, because no one passed it to the agent whose job it was. The structure was there; the handoff was not.

The encouraging part is where the line fell. Every rule I enforced in code held all day: no payment without approval, no agent able to approve its own request, communications saved as drafts only, and every date judged against the shop’s stored date rather than the real one. Every rule I left to the prompt slipped at least once. If I carried on with this, I would move more of the “follow through” into code — make a run refuse to finish without a status update, and make the tools reject an unknown SKU instead of quietly returning zero.

One thing I would change first. Not the agents, the tools. If check_order_fulfillment had refused the Boss’s invented SKU instead of returning a zero, and if update_ticket_status were required before a run could finish, two of the three defects on this page would not exist. The guardrails that were written in code held perfectly all day. The ones left to the prompt are the ones that slipped.
